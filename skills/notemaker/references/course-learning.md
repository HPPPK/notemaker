# Course and slide notes: recover the knowledge, not the timetable

Use the shared section introduction → concept/example pages → closing summary structure. Organize around what the learner should understand and be able to do. A slide deck is a compressed teaching aid, not already a complete explanation.

## Select what matters

First identify substantive concepts, prerequisites, formulas, diagrams, procedures, worked examples and practice questions. Mark title pages, attendance, grade statistics, deadlines and software setup separately. Omit or briefly relocate these unless the user wants course logistics. Do not expand them into the main knowledge outline simply because their text is easy to extract.

If the supplied deck only introduces the course, describe that narrow scope and summarize its actual learning goals. Do not pretend it demonstrates concept-teaching quality. When evaluating this skill, include at least one technical teaching deck with mathematics/diagrams/examples, rather than testing only the first administrative lecture.

## Build a learning progression

For each important concept, connect the motivating problem, precise meaning, mechanism or formula, and a concrete use. Expand the reasoning compressed between slide bullets. Read nearby slides together when a definition, figure and exercise form one argument. Do not make one topic per slide by default.

For a formula, preserve the notation, define its symbols, explain what is computed and why, and state applicable assumptions. Distinguish computational conditions from assumptions needed for statistical interpretation. Show one actual substitution or worked case when useful; avoid replacing explanation with an unexplained formula image.

Use original diagrams where they carry the lesson. Inspect the slide pixels because arrows, axes and formula layout may be missing in text extraction. Explain where to look and what to compare. Newly drawn illustrations must be labeled as additions rather than original experimental evidence.

## Solve something

If the deck poses a central exercise, include a usable solution at the appropriate depth, not just the question. Separate the supplied problem from the note author's derived answer. Show the key intermediate steps, result and a quick check. Verify calculations with available tools. If only part is solved, say which part; do not imply the deck supplies the answer.

Add short misconception Q&A when it addresses the content: e.g. why a curved polynomial can still be linear in its parameters, or why perfect training fit need not predict well. These are teaching questions authored for the note, not invented lecturer statements. Optional revealable solutions can use details/summary cells.

Code is useful when it maps a concept to an actual computation: explain its input, central calculation and output, without a line-by-line tour of imports. Do not insert code into every subject or claim it was run unless it was. Label custom synthetic data.

## Check the lesson

Read the body without the source. Can a learner define the core ideas, interpret the important figure/formula, solve one representative problem and avoid a key misconception? Does the final synthesis connect the ideas and their limits? If the output mostly discusses the course's format, provenance or how to study, it has missed a knowledge-summary request.
